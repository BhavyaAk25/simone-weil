# Sources and quotation register

Quotation register checked on 8 September 2026; biographical sequence and revised narrative checked again on 16 September 2026. The English quotations in the application are short translations prepared for this project by Codex from the linked French text. They are not attributed to an existing English translator. The visible English work titles are reading aids; the French editions below are the editions actually checked.

## Research basis

- A. Rebecca Rozelle-Stone and Benjamin P. Davis, [“Simone Weil,” Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/simone-weil/), substantive revision 18 September 2025: §1 establishes the main chronology; §2 supports the treatment of power, force, and obligations; §§3–6 inform the ideas; §7 distinguishes later reception from the life.
- Eric O. Springsted, [“About Weil,” American Weil Society](https://simoneweilsociety.org/about): a second biographical account, including the asymmetry between Weil’s voluntary factory experience and her colleagues’ circumstances, her spiritual development, and her hostility toward Judaism. Its claim about a deathbed baptism is not treated here as settled fact.
- Bibliothèque nationale de France, [“Simone Weil, la pensée en action”](https://www.bnf.fr/fr/simone-weil-la-pensee-en-action): archival overview of her studies, activism, factory work, wartime activity, notebooks, and manuscripts.
- Institut de France, [“Parcours et œuvres de la philosophe Simone Weil (3/4)”](https://podcasts.institutdefrance.fr/emissions/eclairage/parcours-et-oeuvres-de-la-philosophe-simone-weil-3-4): confirms the provincial teaching sequence and explains why early posthumous editions are editorial constructions, rather than books Weil prepared for publication.
- Bibliothèque nationale de France, [“Lecture de Simone Weil”](https://www.bnf.fr/fr/agenda/lecture-de-simone-weil), 6 February 2023: dates *La Personne et le Sacré* to the final London period.

The paragraph prose is original narrative synthesis. The paper scenes are interpretive compositions, not claims to reconstruct particular rooms, buildings, or meetings. No facts are inferred from the reference mockups, and no words printed in those images are accepted as verified quotations.

## Dates and editorial status

- Spreads 1 and 7 quote a letter from Marseille to Joseph-Marie Perrin, dated approximately 15 May 1942. The accounts of childhood and the 1930s are explicitly retrospective. They describe her own recollection and interpretation.
- Spread 2 uses her later, religious treatment of learning, written in 1941–1942. It must never be presented as something she said as a student in the 1920s.
- Spreads 3 and 5 use the same letter, dated 1934 by the checked edition and written after entering factory work. Two short, distinct excerpts connect her teaching to the change in perspective at the factory.
- The oppression essay in spread 4 was written in 1934. *Oppression et Liberté* appeared after her death in 1955.
- The Bernanos letter is usually dated 1938; some editions retain a question mark. The app therefore says “usually dated,” not an invented exact day. The 2018 volume is the checked reprint, not the date of composition or first publication.
- The Iliad essay was written in 1939–1940 and appeared in *Cahiers du Sud* in December 1940 and January 1941 under Émile Novis. *La Source grecque* is a later collection.
- *Gravity and Grace* first appeared in 1947. Gustave Thibon selected and arranged extracts from notebooks written in 1940–1942. The quotations are checked against the 1948 French edition. The collection is not a systematic book that Weil finished, titled, or organized herself.
- *The Need for Roots* was written in London in 1943 and published posthumously in 1949. “Human Personality” was also written in the final London period; the checked 1957 collection is posthumous.

## Historical handling and reuse

The chronology gives the factory period as 1934–1935 without calling it 12 uninterrupted months of labor. It does not equate voluntary entry with having no alternative. The Spain spread distinguishes a moral criticism of her own side from endorsement of Franco. The spiritual spread reports experiences as Weil’s accounts and leaves the disputed baptism history out. The final illness is described without treating death as an achievement or claiming one uncontested explanation for food restriction. The final spread acknowledges her hostility toward Judaism; admiration for her thought must not erase that hostility.

Use is limited to short attributed quotations, with newly prepared English translations and original project prose. No published English translations, modern introductions, footnotes, illustrations, or complete digital books are bundled. Wikisource explicitly separates Weil’s text from still-protected editorial matter in [Attente de Dieu](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Attente_de_Dieu), [La Pesanteur et la Grâce](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/La_Pesanteur_et_la_Grâce), and [L’Espagnole](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/L’Espagnole). The latter explicitly identifies the included Weil texts as public domain while excluding the modern edition’s preliminary matter and notes. Source-site contribution terms remain separate from rights in the underlying work. Retain author, work, translator description, edition, and links whenever reusing these excerpts.

No claim is made that an arbitrary modern translation or complete edition can be redistributed. The application uses English translations made specifically for this project, not the English wording embedded in the visual reference images.

## Narrative revision, 16 September 2026

All 12 original paragraphs were reread as a continuous biography and rewritten for readers unfamiliar with Weil. Each is 58–61 words. The sequence now connects curiosity to study, study to teaching, teaching to political inquiry, and inquiry to factory experience. The later paragraphs connect war, attention, belonging, her final illness, and the survival of her work. Her moral courage is shown through actions without erasing her contradictions. No quotation text, source locator, translator credit, or retrospective publication note changed.

Checks against the [Stanford chronology](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/simone-weil/#PhilDeve) and the [American Weil Society biography](https://simoneweilsociety.org/about) confirm entry to the École normale supérieure in 1928, teaching from 1931, the German visit in 1932, factory work in 1934–1935, Spain in 1936, the 1942 journey via New York to London, and death on 24 August 1943 at age 34. The route is compressed without implying a direct France-to-New York crossing. The Free French are identified as a resistance movement to orient readers.

Spread 4 explicitly takes place during the teaching years. Spread 7 explicitly runs alongside the political story, with the Portuguese procession in 1935, Assisi in 1937, and Solesmes in 1938. These dates and the experience while reciting a poem are supported by her [Spiritual Autobiography](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Attente_de_Dieu/Autobiographie_spirituelle). That account also supports spread 1’s description of her adolescent doubts and her belief that attention opens the pursuit of truth to everyone.

The discussion of power in spread 4 remains an accessible synthesis of the [Analysis of Oppression](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Oppression_et_Liberté/05/03). The factory paragraph follows her account of mechanical work, speed, and fatigue in the [Letter to a Student](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/La_Condition_ouvrière/03), with the voluntary nature of her experience retained. Spread 8 explains the opening account of force in [The Iliad, or the Poem of Force](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/La_Source_grecque/01/L’Iliade,_ou_le_poème_de_la_force). Spread 10 connects belonging to participation in communities as described at the opening of [The Need for Roots, Part II](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/L’Enracinement/Partie_2).

Spread 11’s closing statement is editorial synthesis, not an additional quotation. Its account of care independent of social status follows the opening argument of [Human Personality](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Écrits_de_Londres_et_dernières_lettres/Écrits_de_Londres/01). The disputed reasons for her food restriction remain explicitly unresolved. The final paragraph preserves the criticism of her judgments about Judaism and identifies posthumous books as the work of friends and editors, rather than claiming she finished or arranged those editions.

Editorial check: 12 paragraphs, 45–65 words each, age written as **34**, dates and quantities in digits, and no em dashes in the runtime chapter manifest. Impersonal “One” in the unchanged Spain quotation is a pronoun, not a numerical quantity. Source identifiers such as Letter IV remain in their bibliographic form.
